# Prove one layered search, not twelve rules

The most principled algorithm is a **forward, slot-by-slot reachability search over the combined state of small rule "checkers"**. In constraint-programming terms it is Pesant's REGULAR layered graph; in dynamic-programming terms it is a 24-step DP. You prove it sound (anything it returns satisfies every rule) and complete (if any valid answer exists, it finds one) **once, generically, in Lean 4 core**. Every rule you listed fits this frame once the LLM's call is fixed, because each call-relative rule then becomes an ordinary rule about the answer. Most rules need no memory at all. The others need only a last pitch, a small counter or a flag, except "transpose the call's first N notes", which needs a remembered offset and should be bounded. A realistic five-rule combination produces **about 3,000 combined states per slot and about 3.5 million transitions over the whole phrase**, which is milliseconds of work. That beats every alternative the music literature offers. Classical music constraint solvers use exponential backtracking, local-search and genetic systems cannot prove that no answer exists, and a SAT/LRAT route would need a proof that the CNF encoding is faithful, which costs more than the whole DP. Budget roughly **200–450 lines of Lean for the framework and its two theorems**, plus per-rule proofs, which will be the real time sink.

## Exact music-constraint precedent converges on one polynomial decider

Music constraint systems fall into three camps, and only one of them gives a fast, exact answer to "does any valid answer exist?". The first camp is the classical constraint-programming systems. PWConstraints/PMC and Score-PMC, Situation, BackTalk and Strasheela all run *complete* search, meaning that if a solution exists the search finds it, but they get there by backtracking with optional propagation. PWConstraints "performs a chronological backtracking (BT) search … BT performs a complete search", so the worst case is exponential ([Anders & Miranda, ACM CSUR 2011](https://cm-mail.stanford.edu/pipermail/cmdist/attachments/20111228/c7bd1856/attachment.pdf)). The second camp is heuristic: OMClouds is "based solely on a heuristic search strategy" and "may never find a true all-interval series" even when one exists (same survey). The variable-neighbourhood counterpoint generator of Herremans & Sörensen ([SUTD repository](https://repository.sutd.edu.sg/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Composing-first-species-counterpoint-with-a/9911708209846)) and GenJam's interactive genetic algorithm, which trades fours with a live soloist ([Biles, ICMC'98](https://genjam.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/07/bilesicmc98.pdf)), also belong here. When these optimizers fail to find an answer, that failure proves nothing. They are fine as *generators* behind an exact gate, never as the gate itself.

The third camp, Pachet and Roy's Markov-constraints line together with Seshia's Control Improvisation, is where exactness becomes cheap. Pachet, Roy and Barbieri showed that when every constraint is either **unary** (about one position) or **binary between adjacent positions**, the constraint network is a chain. Making it arc-consistent then yields backtrack-free generation, which means every partial melody that survives the filtering can be extended to a full one ([IJCAI 2011](https://www.ijcai.org/Proceedings/11/Papers/113.pdf)). Their musical "Answer" constraint, in which the last note equals the first input note, is a direct call-and-response precedent (same paper). Richer rules were then compiled into automata. For example, the anti-plagiarism MaxOrder constraint became a REGULAR constraint whose filtering runs "in O(n.T) time" and supports exact solution counting ([Papadopoulos, Roy, Pachet, AAAI 2014](https://object.cloud.sdsc.edu/v1/AUTH_da4962d3368042ac8337e2dfdd3e7bf3/ml-papers-txt/AAAI/2014/avoiding_plagiarism_in_markov_sequence_generation__15f5df81.txt)). Meter and cardinality rules get pseudo-polynomial filtering, meaning the cost grows with the size of the numbers involved ([Roy & Pachet, AAAI 2013](https://ojs.aaai.org/index.php/AAAI/article/view/8661)). Control Improvisation formalizes improvisations as bounded-length words accepted by a hard specification. When the specifications are deterministic automata the problem is polynomial, and with nondeterministic automata or general grammars it becomes #P-hard ([Fremont, Donzé, Seshia, arXiv 1704.06319](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1704.06319)). That hardness, however, comes from the *counting and randomness* requirements. Your question involves only a hard specification and asks only whether a solution exists, which reduces to bounded-length non-emptiness and stays polynomial.

The known ways to lose tractability are equally clear. Rivaud & Pachet show that sampling under equality constraints between arbitrary, non-adjacent positions is #P-complete in general ([arXiv 1711.10436](https://arxiv.org/abs/1711.10436), a preprint with a sketched proof). Constraints that tie together far-apart answer notes are therefore the danger zone. One important caveat applies to the whole section: **no formally verified (Coq, Lean or Isabelle) music constraint solver turned up**, so a verified decider for call-and-response rules appears to be new territory.

| System / approach | Search style | Can prove "no answer exists"? | Worst-case cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| PWConstraints, Score-PMC, Situation, BackTalk, Strasheela | Backtracking ± propagation | Yes (complete) | Exponential |
| OMClouds, VNS counterpoint, GenJam | Local / evolutionary search | No (heuristic) | Not applicable |
| Markov constraints on a chain, REGULAR automaton | Arc consistency / layered graph | Yes (complete) | Polynomial in automaton size |
| Control Improvisation, DFA specifications | Counting on automata | Yes | Polynomial (#P-hard with NFAs or CFGs when counting) |

## A checker per rule and a product that stays near 3,000 states

Three plain-language terms do all the work. A **checker** (formally a deterministic finite automaton) reads the answer one slot at a time, left to right, and keeps a small memory called its *state*: "the last pitch I heard", "notes played so far in this bar". After the 24th slot it says yes or no. Represent the answer as 24 symbols, each either *rest* or *onset at pitch p* (p in 40..88), which gives an alphabet of 50 symbols. The **product** of several checkers is just running them side by side: the combined state is the tuple of their states, and the combined checker says yes only when all of them do. It accepts exactly the answers that satisfy every rule ([closure of DFAs under intersection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deterministic_finite_automaton); Mathlib's `DFA.inter` and `accepts_inter` formalize this ([Mathlib docs](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/Computability/DFA.html))). **Sound and complete** means the search never returns an invalid answer and never says "impossible" when an answer exists.

The search itself is Pesant's layered graph. Copy the product's states once per slot, draw an arc for each symbol allowed at that slot, and a valid answer is exactly a path from the start to an accepting state at slot 24. Paths and solutions "are in one-to-one correspondence", and the whole graph is processed in O(n·|δ|), where |δ| is the number of transitions ([van Hoeve, Pesant, Rousseau 2004](https://arxiv.org/pdf/cs/0408023); [Pesant, Quimper, Zanarini, JAIR 2012](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1401.4601)). In practice you **never build the product**. You keep a set of reachable combined states for each slot, extend every state by every allowed symbol, and deduplicate. Deduplication is what turns 50^24 ≈ 6×10^40 candidate melodies into a few thousand states per slot. Two details make this framing fit your app especially well. First, the slot index is free memory: the search knows which slot it is on, so "beat 3 of bar 2" or "reset the bar counter at slot 12" costs no extra states. Second, the call is fixed before the search runs, so "echo the call's rhythm" collapses into "slot i must hold an onset if and only if the call has one there". That is a per-slot filter, not memory. Beldiceanu, Carlsson and Petit showed that checkers with symbolic counters lose the exactness guarantee ([summarized in Raiser 2008](https://www.uni-ulm.de/fileadmin/website_uni_ulm/iui.inst.170/home/raiser/publications/Raiser2008a.pdf)). That warning does not apply here, because every counter is bounded by 24 and can be written into the state explicitly.

The table below is derived from elementary counting rather than literature benchmarks. "LP" means *last sounded pitch*, which has 50 values (49 pitches plus "none yet"). One LP field can be shared by every rule that needs it.

| Rule | What the checker remembers | Extra states | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| Echo the call's rhythm (exact) | Nothing: slot i allows an onset iff the call has one at i | 1 | Fits cleanly |
| Echo rhythm loosely ("share ≥ K onset slots") | Shared-onset count, saturating at K | K+1 (≤ 25) | Fits |
| Transpose/sequence the call's first N notes | Notes placed so far (0..N) × offset d, fixed by the first note | ≈ 1 + 49·N + 1 (N=4 → 198) | Needs restriction (bound N; bound the offset) |
| Contrary motion | Phase {no note, one note at pitch p, done}; p is the shared LP | ≈ 51, mostly shared with LP | Fits. The required direction is computed from the call at build time |
| Play fewer notes than the call | Onset count, must stay ≤ call count − 1 | ≤ call count (e.g. 8) | Fits |
| Start within K semitones of the call's last note | "Has a note sounded yet?" flag | 2 | Fits |
| Max leap L | LP | 50 | Fits |
| Chord tone on a given beat (onset version) | Nothing: restricts allowed symbols at that slot; chord comes from the 12-bar form | 1 | Fits cleanly |
| Chord tone *sounding* on a beat (held note counts) | LP (shared) | 0 extra if LP already present | Needs restriction (define "sounding" precisely) |
| Avoid a pitch class | Nothing | 1 | Fits cleanly |
| Stay in a range | Nothing | 1 | Fits cleanly |
| At most N notes per bar | Per-bar counter, reset at slot 12 | N+1 | Fits |
| Syncopation (≥ 1 off-beat onset) | "Seen an off-beat?" flag | 2 | Fits |
| Leave space (no onsets in first K slots) | Nothing | 1 | Fits cleanly |

Multiplying everything naively overstates the cost badly. For max leap + transpose (N=4) + fewer-than-call (call has 8 notes) + ≤4 per bar + chord tone, the raw product is 50 × 198 × 8 × 5 ≈ 396,000 states per slot. Sharing collapses it. While the transposition is active, the last pitch and the note count are *determined* by (notes placed, offset), and once the transposition finishes the offset is dead weight and can be dropped. The result is **at most about 3,000 reachable states per slot, about 7×10^4 nodes and about 3.5×10^6 transitions** across all 24 slots. A max-leap rule cuts the transitions further, because each state can only move to 2L+1 pitches. These numbers are my own arithmetic, not a published benchmark. A useful rule of thumb follows: **the state count is roughly the product of the ranges of everything remembered at the same time, so keep it under about 10^6 per slot.**

The search also detects contradictions between rules automatically, which is worth showing in the UI. Exact rhythm echo plus "fewer notes than the call" is always unsatisfiable, since the onset count is forced equal and also required to be strictly smaller. Transposing N ≥ 2 notes copies the call's first interval, which can contradict contrary motion. "Start within K" plus transposition limits the offset to at most 2K+1 values. A backward pass over the same layers (which states can still reach acceptance) gives Pesant-style domain consistency for free, so the app can **grey out notes that cannot lead to any valid answer**. Counting paths instead of testing existence ("1.2 M answers fit your rules") or picking the best-scoring path costs the same pass ([Pesant, Quimper, Zanarini](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1401.4601)).

## Restrict the transposition rule and refuse self-referential rules

Only one of your proposed rules is risky. "Transpose/sequence the call's first N notes" ties answer notes 1..N to a single unknown offset. In general that is the equality-between-non-adjacent-positions pattern Rivaud & Pachet flag as hard ([arXiv 1711.10436](https://arxiv.org/abs/1711.10436)). It becomes harmless here because the pitch range is bounded. You can store the offset in the state (fixed at the first note, at most 49 values), or enumerate offsets as a finite disjunction and run one search per offset. Either way the cost multiplies by roughly 50–100. To keep it cheap, cap N at about 4, drop the offset from the state once note N is placed, and, if the player also picked "start within K", let that rule cut the offsets down to 2K+1. "Sequence" (repeating a shape at a different pitch level) should be defined as the same intervals from a new starting pitch. That definition is exactly the same checker.

The rules to avoid share one trait: they compare the answer *with itself* across unbounded distances, so the checker would have to remember a large set of past values. "No repeated motif anywhere" requires remembering every m-note fragment seen so far, which grows exponentially. The restricted form "no immediate repetition" costs 49^m states (about 2.4k for m=2 and 118k for m=3). "All pitches distinct" needs 2^49 states; restricted to pitch classes it needs 4,096, which is acceptable. "The answer is a palindrome of itself" is context-free rather than regular in general and needs the GRAMMAR constraint, cubic-time CYK parsing ([Katsirelos et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/0906.5233); [Isabelle AFP CYK](https://isa-afp.org/entries/CYK.html)). Average-pitch or total-duration targets need a sum counter of about 2,100 values, which is tolerable alone but multiplies everything else. The counter-intuitive good news is that **comparisons against the call are almost always cheap**. "Not a copy of the call" is a 2-state flag, "retrograde of the call" is a per-slot filter, and "the call does not appear as a contiguous fragment" is a string-matching automaton with (call notes + 1) states. When a player-facing rule sounds global, rephrase it relative to the fixed call before reaching for a solver.

## Prove the DP once in Lean and skip LRAT

Three verification routes exist, and the comparison is lopsided. The **LRAT route** encodes the problem as a SAT formula, lets an external solver produce an unsatisfiability proof, and checks that proof with Lean core's verified checker. The signature is `Std.Tactic.BVDecide.LRAT.check … : Bool`, with `check_sound … : check lratProof cnf = true → cnf.Unsat` ([Lean API](https://lean-lang.org/doc/api/Std/Tactic/BVDecide/LRAT/Checker.html)). The checker is reusable, but you would still have to prove that roughly 24 × 50 one-hot variables plus every rule's clauses mean exactly the rules. That encoding proof dwarfs the DP. `bv_decide` also routes through `Lean.ofReduceBool`, which puts the compiler in the trusted base, and it proves only "no solution exists", never handing back a checked witness ([Lean reference: Axioms](https://lean-lang.org/doc/reference/4.25.0/Axioms/)). The **certificate route** has the search output its layers R_0..R_24 and a small Bool checker confirm that the start state is in R_0, that every step from R_k lands in R_{k+1}, and that no state in R_24 accepts. That checker is short (an estimated 40–80 lines). But the DP's layers *already are* this certificate by construction, so proving the DP complete is almost the same proof. A certificate alone also cannot give you the theorem "if an answer exists, search returns one". The **DP route** proves that theorem directly and is the one to choose.

The design is copied from Mathlib's `DFA` file, which defines evaluation as `List.foldl step` and the product as `inter` ([Mathlib docs](https://leanprover-community.github.io/mathlib4_docs/Mathlib/Computability/DFA.html)), and re-implemented in core with a computable `accept : σ → Bool`:

```lean
structure Checker (Sym σ : Type) where
  init : σ
  step : σ → Sym → σ          -- add a Nat slot argument for beat/bar-aware rules
  accept : σ → Bool

def Checker.run (c : Checker Sym σ) (w : List Sym) : σ := w.foldl c.step c.init

def Checker.inter (c₁ : Checker Sym σ₁) (c₂ : Checker Sym σ₂) : Checker Sym (σ₁ × σ₂) :=
  ⟨(c₁.init, c₂.init), fun (s₁, s₂) a => (c₁.step s₁ a, c₂.step s₂ a),
   fun (s₁, s₂) => c₁.accept s₁ && c₂.accept s₂⟩
```

Pair each checker with a `spec : List Sym → Prop` and a lemma `correct : ∀ w, checker accepts w ↔ spec w`, and combine rules by nesting `inter`. The state is then a nested product with `DecidableEq` derived automatically, so you never need a heterogeneous list of rules. The DP `layer : Nat → List (σ × List Sym)` recurses *structurally* on k. It extends every (state, prefix) pair by every symbol in an explicit `alphabet` (backed by a lemma that every symbol is in it) and deduplicates by state with a hand-written `insertNew`. `search` then picks an accepting entry and **re-validates the witness before returning it**, which makes soundness a roughly five-line proof that does not depend on the DP being correct. Completeness follows from a single invariant proved by induction on k. (a) *Genuineness*: every (s, p) in layer k has `p.length = k` and `run p = s`. (b) *Coverage*: every prefix p of length k has some entry `(run p, q)` in layer k. Two small facts about `foldl insertNew` support it, each proved with the accumulator generalized: outputs come from inputs, and every input state appears in the output. State the result as `(∃ w, w.length = n ∧ spec w) → (search c alphabet n).isSome`, prove it for generic n, and instantiate 24 at the end.

The pitfalls are concrete and avoidable. **Always `induction l generalizing acc`** for foldl lemmas, the most common stumbling block ([arXiv 2608.09277](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.09277)). Avoid `partial` and well-founded recursion, because since Lean 4.9 well-founded definitions are irreducible by default and `decide`/`rfl` stall on them ([v4.9.0 release notes](https://lean-lang.org/doc/reference/stable/releases/v4.9.0/)). Do not try to prove a specific instance with kernel `decide`: 24 layers × thousands of states × 50 symbols will time out. `native_decide` adds a compiler-trust axiom visible in `#print axioms` ([Lean reference 4.30](https://lean-lang.org/doc/reference/4.30.0-rc1/Axioms/)). The honest demo is `#eval search …` backed by the general theorem. Write rule specs recursively (pattern matching on `a :: b :: rest`) rather than with `Fin`-indexed `w[i]` accesses, which are much harder to connect to a fold. Expect lemma names to have drifted (`bind` → `flatMap`, `join` → `flatten`) and let `exact?`/`simp?` find the current ones. Core's `Std.HashMap`/`HashSet` now ship real lemma libraries, but they require `EquivBEq` and `LawfulHashable` and cause friction around `toList` ([HashSet lemmas](https://lean-lang.org/doc/api/Std/Data/HashSet/Lemmas.html)). The pragmatic split is a proven List DP plus an *unproven* HashMap fast path, cross-checked at runtime and never claimed as verified. That split matters more than it looks. List deduplication is quadratic in the layer size, and at about 3,000 states per layer times 50 symbols my rough arithmetic puts the List version at billions of comparisons over a full search: fine for a few hundred states and slow for thousands. Keep the proven path's rule sets lean, or demo with the fast path under the cross-check. The `leanprover/human-eval-lean` repo is a good style model for core-only verified algorithms that add missing lemmas inline ([GitHub](https://github.com/leanprover/human-eval-lean)).

For calibration, a hand-built CYK verification took about 1,000 lines of Isabelle ([Wimmer, Hu, Nipkow](https://home.cit.tum.de/~wimmers/papers/Memoization_DP.pdf)), and a whole constructive theory of regular languages took about 1,400 lines of Coq ([Doczkal, Kaiser, Smolka, CPP 2013](https://www.ps.uni-saarland.de/Publications/documents/DoczkalEtAl_2013_A-Constructive.pdf)). Bounded-length emptiness of a single product automaton is a far narrower slice. **About 200–450 lines and 0.5–1.5 days** for a competent Lean user with AI assistance is a reasonable estimate for the framework, though it is judgement, not a published figure. No existing Lean 4 library ships a verified bounded-length emptiness-with-witness procedure, so this part is yours to write.

Use the backward pass to grey out impossible notes.

## Conclusion

Verifying the search is less of a trap than it looks, provided the rules are modeled as left-to-right checkers rather than arbitrary predicates. The rule list then determines only the state count, and the correctness proof stays fixed. Fixing the call before the search is the move that makes the whole thing tractable: it turns "relative to the call" into "about one slot" for nearly every rule, so the only real design debt is the transposition offset. That suggests a product principle as well as a proof strategy. Offer players rules that compare against the call, which are cheap and verifiable, and steer them away from rules that compare the answer with itself, which blow up the state. And because the same verified layers also count solutions and flag dead-end notes, one proven artifact can drive the solver, the hint UI and the "these two rules conflict" message. The open risk is engineering, not theory: whether a proven List DP stays fast enough at thousands of states per slot, and how long the per-rule spec proofs take, which no source here could estimate.
